False WhatsApp Viral
Original Viral Claim

"Drinking high-concentration boiled ginger water on an empty stomach dissolves kidney stones completely in 48 hours without surgery."

Why Boiled Ginger Water Does Not Melt Kidney Stones

While ginger contains antioxidants and anti-inflammatory gingerols, kidney stones are calcified crystalline deposits (calcium oxalate, uric acid). Ingested ginger compounds are metabolized in the digestive tract and cannot chemically dissolve hard mineral stones in renal tubules.

Sources: American Urological Association, PubMed Read Full Investigation →

The Clinical Reality

Delaying medical intervention for obstructive nephrolithiasis based on home concoctions can lead to hydronephrosis, severe infection, and irreversible renal damage.

Key Takeaway for Patients: Stone passage depends on diameter, location, and urinary hydration—not herbal melting. Consult a qualified urologist if experiencing acute flank pain.

Misleading Nutrition & Diet
The Claim

"Drinking alkaline water balances body pH and cures acid-based diseases."

Can Alkaline Water Actually Alter Blood pH Levels?

Human blood pH is tightly regulated between 7.35 and 7.45 by renal and respiratory systems. Stomach acid instantly neutralizes alkaline fluids before absorption.

About the Platform

Why Science-Backed Fact-Checking Matters

Health misinformation is not harmless gossip. Misleading medical advice leads people to ingest toxic concoctions, abandon life-saving therapies, and delay critical interventions. Myth Rx evaluates viral health rumors against indexed clinical literature, producing clear, peer-reviewed takeaways.

Our 4-Step Methodology → Editorial Standards

Standardized Verdict System

Every claim investigated on Myth Rx is classified into one of four clear tiers:

  • Supported — Scientific consensus & clinical evidence back the claim.
  • False — Demonstrably incorrect, fabricated, or physiologically impossible.
  • Misleading — Partial lab truth distorted into unproven health advice.
  • Unverified — Insufficient human trials to confirm or refute efficacy.
